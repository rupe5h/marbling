What is marbling

Marbling is a way of decorating a surface with colors floated on liquid. Paint or ink is dropped onto a bath, either plain water or a size thickened with carrageenan from seaweed. A little surfactant (traditionally ox gall) makes each drop spread into a thin disc that pushes the earlier colors outward into rings. The marbler then drags a stylus, rake or comb through the floating colors. Finally a sheet of paper or cloth, treated with alum so the pigment sticks, is laid on the surface and lifted off with the pattern on it. Every print is one of a kind. [4]

The oldest known tradition is Japanese suminagashi (“floating ink”): marbled papers appear in a 12th-century anthology of poems, the Sanjūrokunin-shū. Marbling on a thickened bath developed in Persia and Central Asia by the late 15th century and became the Turkish art of ebru. It reached Europe in the 17th century, and marbled papers became the classic covers and endpapers of bound books. [4] [1]

Why marbling can be computed exactly

If the marbler moves slowly, the floating layer of paint behaves as a two-dimensional, incompressible, viscous (Newtonian) fluid. A rake stretches and folds the boundaries between colors but never tears them or changes how they connect. A raking can even be undone by drawing the rake back to where it started. Mathematically, each motion is a homeomorphism: a continuous map of the surface onto itself with a continuous inverse. Many marbling motions can be written as exact, closed-form maps of this kind. [3]

What is digital marbling

One way to marble on a computer is to simulate the fluid: solve the equations of fluid flow on a grid and carry the colors along. That is general but slow, and the colors blur as they are moved from cell to cell. Lu, Jaffer, Jin, Zhao and Mao took another route in Mathematical Marbling [1]. They describe each marbling operation as a closed-form formula that says where every point of the surface moves to. Those formulas compose and can be inverted, they are easy to compute in parallel, and they stay sharp at any resolution. Jaffer later derived exact formulas for single stylus strokes and for vortices from the physics of slow viscous flow, which is orders of magnitude faster than finite-element simulation.

How this app works

  • Everything you do is recorded as an action in a log: a drop, a rake, a stir, and so on. Each action is one of those invertible maps.
  • To color a pixel, the GPU walks it backward through the log, undoing the motions one by one. If it lands inside a drop at the moment that drop was made, it takes the drop's color. Otherwise it ends up showing the background. pst-marble renders the same way [3]. Colors come straight from the drops instead of being moved from pixel to pixel, so a render is exact at any size, and Export PNG renders the tank afresh at up to 4096 × 4096 pixels.
  • While you paint, the tank is kept as a pair of oversampled textures. Each gesture applies only its new actions on top of the previous state, so a long history doesn't slow it down. A stroke in progress resamples that state every frame to keep up with the pointer. When it ends, it is re-rendered exactly, and whenever you pause, a supersampled render of the whole log is refined in the background, a strip at a time, without blocking input.
  • Undo and redo replay the log, with an exact render saved every 200 actions as a checkpoint. Export preset saves the log as JSON, which Import preset (in the gallery) plays back step by step.
  • Units follow pst-marble: the tank is 1 m square, so lengths in the settings are in millimetres, speeds in mm/s and viscosity in mm²/s. Settings from pst-marble's examples therefore carry over unchanged, and several of its examples are among the built-in presets.

How the tools work

On a computer the tools are in a drawer on the left: the tab on its edge opens and closes it, and you can keep working with it closed. On a phone they sit below the tank. Each picture below was rendered by the app from a few actions. The usage notes and settings listed under each tool come straight from the tool itself.

Paint

The Paint button opens a menu of the ways to drop paint. Patterns cycle through the palette in order, or use only the selected color when Cycle colors is off.

Drop

A new drop of radius r at c keeps its own color and pushes all the paint around it outward. The pushed paint keeps its area, so a circle around the drop grows to enclose the drop's area as well [1]:

p′ = c + (p − c) · √(1 + r² / |p − c|²)

Far from the drop, almost nothing moves. Close to it, earlier drops are squeezed into thin crescents.

Grid

Drops in rows and columns, dropped one after another, so later drops push earlier ones out of round. Serpentine order runs alternate rows backward, as pst-marble's serpentine-drops does, and Jitter nudges every drop by a random amount.

Concentric

Drops are dropped on the same spot, largest first, with radii t·√(2n+1). Each new drop pushes the earlier ones out into a ring, and these radii make every ring the same thickness t. This is pst-marble's concentric-rings, the usual starting point for the deformation tools.

Line

Evenly spaced drops between two points (pst-marble's line-drops).

Coil

Drops along a spiral (pst-marble's coil-drops). Each drop is one arc step along the curve from the last, and the spiral's radius grows by the radius step each time.

Uniform

Drops scattered uniformly at random over a rectangle (pst-marble's uniform-drops), like paint flicked from a brush. This is the basis of the traditional “stone” patterns.

Normal

Drops in a Gaussian cloud, thickest at the center, with spreads σx and σy (pst-marble's normal-drops). Positions come from the Box–Muller transform.

The random patterns are seeded (Random seed, under Advanced options), so the preview you see while hovering is exactly what drops. The seed advances after each pattern.

Deform

The other tools move the paint that is already on the bath. How far a motion reaches depends on the bath's viscosity and on the speed and diameter of the tine, rod or stylus. For a rake the influence falls off over a length L = V·D² / ν: a faster or thicker tine drags paint over a wider band, and a more viscous bath keeps the disturbance closer to the tine. Viscosity is under Advanced options.

Stylus

A single pointed tool drawn through the paint, used for the short strokes of flowers and other pictures. The flow around a slowly moving stylus is Oseen flow, and Jaffer gives its exact displacement field. A stroke is applied in steps no longer than L. Slow viscous flow is reversible, so the stroke is undone by running it backward.

Rake

A row of evenly spaced tines pulled straight across the tank. Every point moves in the direction of the rake, by the distance the rake travels times a sum of one term per tine:

shift = travel · Σtines e−(distance to tine) / L

Paint right at a tine moves the whole way, and paint between tines lags behind, which draws out the familiar combed points [1]. Angle snap keeps the direction to round angles.

Stir

A circular rake: rods on circles around a center, all turned through the angle you sweep. Paint on a rod's circle turns by that angle, and the turn fades with relative distance from the circle. Angular speed and rod diameter play the roles that speed and tine diameter play for a rake. With several rods the rings in between are dragged along at different rates.

Vortex

A spinning whirl in the bath that slows as viscosity drains its energy: a Lamb–Oseen vortex. Jaffer found a closed form for how far it turns the paint at distance r, given its circulation Γ and how long it runs (t):

θ(r) = Γ / ((4ν)3/4 + (2πr² / t)3/4)4/3

The center turns the most, and the turn tapers off outward, winding drops into spirals. Real marbling baths are usually too viscous for true vortices, which is why they are rare in traditional work.

Jiggle

A sinusoidal shake of the whole tank. Along the drag direction u, the paint at phase φ = k(p·u) moves by A sin φ along u and by B cos φ across it. The across amplitude (the drag length) turns straight bands into waves. With both amplitudes set, each point is displaced around an ellipse. Undoing it means solving φ + kA sin φ = φ′, so kA is kept below 1 to keep the map invertible.

Wriggle

Jiggle in polar coordinates around a center. Each ring of radius r turns by B cos(kr) and moves outward by A sin(kr), so neighboring rings twist back and forth in opposite directions.

Shift

Slides the whole bath without deforming it: every point moves by the same offset d.

p′ = p + d

Paint that leaves the tank over one edge is still in the log, so shifting back brings it back. The paint that comes in over the other edge is whatever the drops pushed out past it. pst-marble's shift places a design on the page this way, and several of the presets use it to center theirs.

The tank

  • Colors: the palette (pick a swatch, change it, add or remove colors) and the background, which is the color of the bath wherever no paint has landed.
  • Undo / Redo (Ctrl/Cmd+Z, Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+Z) step through whole gestures. Clear empties the tank, after asking, since it can't be undone.
  • Export PNG renders the tank exactly at 1024, 2048 or 4096 pixels square. Export preset saves it as JSON to share or import later.
  • Presets: the gallery. Picking a preset plays it back step by step, with each rake, stir and vortex drawn on the tank as it sweeps in. Click the tank or press Esc to skip to the end. Open the tools afterward to keep working on it.

Code

  • pst-marble [3]. Aubrey Jaffer wrote the marbling actions in PostScript (pst-marble.pro), and Jürgen Gilg and Manuel Luque turned them into a PSTricks package for LaTeX. This app is a port of that code to TypeScript and WebGPU:
    • the actions and their parameters and units (src/marble/actions.ts);
    • the forward and inverse maps (src/marble/math.ts, and the WGSL compute shader src/shaders/marble.wgsl, which colors pixels the way pst-marble's actions2rgb does);
    • the drop patterns (src/marble/patterns.ts) and the default palette;
    • the presets Bouquet, Contour, Curl, Eggcrate, Latte, Leaves, Moiré, Nautilus, Nonpareil, Rollers, Spanish wave and Wreath, from pst-marble's example files.
    Each ported source file credits the authors and license and names the pst-marble procedures it comes from. This is a modified port, not pst-marble itself: the changes are listed under Credits in the project's README, and problems with it belong to this project, not to the pst-marble authors.
  • Built with TypeScript, WebGPU and Vite, and tested with Vitest.
